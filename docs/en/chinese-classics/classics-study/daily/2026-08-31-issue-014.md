## Reading guide (English)

### Large Intestine Meridian (Hand-Yangming) — 传导之官

Focus: the meridian that begins where the Lung Meridian leaves off (at the index fingertip) and ascends to the face, completing the "Lung → Large Intestine → Stomach" flow.

- "The Large Intestine meridian of Hand-Yangming begins at the outer tip of the index finger, passes through Hegu (合谷), ascends along the outer forearm to the elbow, up the outer upper arm to the shoulder, enters the supraclavicular fossa, connects with the lung, passes through the diaphragm, and belongs to the large intestine." The pathway mirrors the Lung Meridian (inner forearm) on the outer forearm — a left-right symmetry.
- A branch ascends from the supraclavicular fossa through the neck and cheek into the teeth, wraps around the mouth, crosses at the philtrum (left to right, right to left), and rises to the side of the nostrils — where it meets the Stomach Meridian.
- "When diseased: toothache, cheek swelling." The meridian traverses the cheek and dental region, making it the primary meridian for dental and facial pain.
- "It governs diseases of fluids: yellow eyes, dry mouth, nasal congestion/bleeding, sore throat, shoulder and upper-arm pain, index finger pain and immobility." Excess → heat and swelling along the pathway; deficiency → chills that do not resolve.
- Conceptual hint: "Large Intestine is the official of transmission" (传导之官). Its function is "transformation and conveyance" — receiving waste from the Small Intestine and transforming it for discharge. In daily life, many problems are "transmission failures" — information bottlenecks, emotional backlogs, task pile-ups. Keeping the channel flowing is healthier than clearing after accumulation.

### Dao De Jing — Chapter 14

Focus: the chapter on the three "cannot-be-perceived" qualities of Dao and the reversal into "grasp the ancient Dao to govern the present."

- "Looked for but not seen, it is called Yi (夷). Listened for but not heard, it is called Xi (希). Grasped for but not obtained, it is called Wei (微)." Three primary senses — sight, hearing, touch — all fail to capture Dao. Not because Dao is absent, but because it transcends sensory objects.
- "These three cannot be further investigated; they merge into one." Dao is not an object to be disassembled — it is an indivisible whole.
- "Above it is not bright; below it is not dark. Continuous and unnameable, it returns to no-thing. This is called the form of the formless, the image of the imageless — this is called huǎng huāng (惚恍)." Dao is not nothing, but it has no fixed, graspable form — you cannot hold it, but you can sense it.
- "Meet it — you cannot see its head; follow it — you cannot see its tail." Dao is without beginning or end, transcending time.
- "Hold to the ancient Dao, to govern the present 'having.' To know the primordial beginning — this is called the discipline of Dao (道纪)." The reversal: precisely because Dao transcends senses and time, grasping it lets you connect past and present, and govern the visible world.

Conceptual hint: what truly governs things — patterns, trends, underlying structures — is invisible, inaudible, intangible. Don't rely only on what you can see. Train yourself to perceive the "form of the formless" — the subtle, structural, trend-level signals that underlie visible phenomena.

### Huangdi Sijing — Jingfa · Daofa (经法·道法第一)

Focus: the opening chapter of the *Huangdi Sijing* (Mawangdui silk manuscript, Huang-Lao text), which states "Dao gives birth to Fa (法, law/standard)" — pulling Dao from personal cultivation (as in the *Dao De Jing*) into the realm of institutional governance.

- "Dao gives birth to Fa. Fa is the line that measures gain and loss, clarifies crooked and straight." Unlike the *Dao De Jing*, which keeps Dao largely in personal cultivation, the *Huangdi Sijing* pulls Dao into statecraft — Dao becomes the foundation of law.
- "He who holds the Dao generates Fa and dares not violate it; once Fa is established he dares not abolish it." The lawgiver must first be subject to the law. The authority of law comes from the lawgiver's own seriousness, not from external force.
- "Life has harm — called desire, called not-knowing-enough. Action has harm — called not-timely. Affairs have harm — called going-against, called not-matching. Speech has harm — called untruthful, called boasting." A systematic risk-identification framework from inner (desire) to outer (speech).
- "Misfortune and fortune share the same Dao; none knows where they arise. The way of seeing-and-knowing is only emptiness-and-presence (虚无有)… once form-and-name (形名) are established, the distinction of black and white is settled." This is the origin of the *Huangdi Sijing*'s "form-and-name" doctrine: define things accurately first, and right and wrong become clear.
- "He who holds the Dao, in viewing the world, has no power (埶), no fixed place, no forced action, no selfishness." Four "no's" — the governing posture.
- "The constant standard for governing the people: remove the private and establish the public (去私而立公)." The ultimate destination of "Dao gives birth to Fa" — the spirit of law is public, not private.
- "He who holds the Dao… does not act as master… only then can he be the correct standard for the world." The highest governance is not "to be master" but "to be correct."

Conceptual hint: three most usable ideas — (1) *Dao is the root of Fa*: institutions must be grounded in genuine understanding of how things work, not arbitrary; (2) *form-and-name first, then measurement*: define things accurately and set standards before governing — in any organization, clarify "who does what, what counts as done well" before measuring; (3) *public over private*: rules that serve the public good have lasting power; rules that serve private interest do not.
