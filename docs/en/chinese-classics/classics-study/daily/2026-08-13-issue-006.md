## Reading guide (English)

### Small Intestine Meridian (Hand-Taiyang)

Focus: the yang meridian that takes over from the Heart meridian at the little finger. The Small Intestine meridian starts at the outer side of the little finger tip, runs up the posterior-lateral side of the forearm and upper arm, circles the scapula, enters the chest to connect the heart, passes through the diaphragm to the stomach, and finally "belongs to" the small intestine. Two branches then ascend to the cheek, the outer canthus (entering the ear), and the inner canthus (where it meets the Bladder meridian at Jingming).

Conceptual hint: the route is the body's "sorting and distinguishing" line. The small intestine's classical function is "separating the clear from the turbid" (分清别浊). The meridian's long path from the fingers, through the shoulder and neck, to the eyes and ears suggests that clarity at the sense organs depends on proper sorting all the way down the line.

### Dao De Jing — Chapter 6

Focus: the "valley spirit" (谷神) as the inexhaustible source.

- "The valley spirit does not die; it is called the mysterious female (玄牝)." The valley is empty yet receptive; its "spirit" is the ceaseless generative function. "Mysterious female" names that function as a dark, womb-like origin.
- "The gate of the mysterious female is called the root of heaven and earth." Heaven and earth are not self-grounded; they issue from this "gate."
- "Continuous, as if present; use it without forcing it." The root's action is soft, prolonged, and inexhaustible — not produced by willful strain.

Conceptual hint: this is the chapter of "leave empty space." Sustainable creativity and vitality come from staying connected to an inexhaustible source, not from packing every moment full. The more you force, the sooner you exhaust; the more you allow space, the more the source can keep filling it.

### Kṣitigarbha Sutra — Section 6 (The Buddha praises Kṣitigarbha)

Focus: the Buddha himself radiates light and sound to praise Kṣitigarbha, then entrusts the sutra to bodhisattvas, devas, dragons, ghosts, and spirits for protection after his own parinirvāṇa.

- The Buddha's whole body emits great light and a great voice, addressing all worlds: "Hear me today praise Kṣitigarbha Bodhisattva, who manifests inconceivable powers of compassion and rescues all who suffer from karma." He asks the assembly to "protect this sutra by every expedient means so that all beings may attain the bliss of nirvāṇa."
- Puguang Bodhisattva then asks on behalf of future beings how to benefit from Kṣitigarbha. The Buddha replies that merely hearing Kṣitigarbha's name, joining palms, praising, prostrating, or cherishing the thought of him can transcend the karma of thirty kalpas.
- Making images, offering music, flowers, incense, or even encouraging one other person to do so, brings the protection of hundreds of thousands of spirits day and night.
- If in dreams one sees grieving or fearful spirits, these are likely past relatives — parents, siblings, spouses — fallen into evil destinies and seeking help. The remedy is to read the sutra, make offerings, and dedicate the merit so they may leave those destinies.
- The Buddha gives three names for the sutra: "Kṣitigarbha Original Vows," "Kṣitigarbha Original Practices," and "Kṣitigarbha Original Vow-Power Sutra," because the bodhisattva made great vows long ago to benefit beings.

Conceptual hint: Section 6 shifts the sutra from "Kṣitigarbha tells his own story" to "the whole cosmos holds up this vow." A great vow is not a solo act; it requires a field of protectors, transmitters, practitioners, and beneficiaries. The practical lesson: when your burden feels too heavy, the problem may be that you are trying to carry a shared vow alone.
