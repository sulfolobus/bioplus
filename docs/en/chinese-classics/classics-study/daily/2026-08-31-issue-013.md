## Reading guide (English)

### Huangdi Sijing (黄帝四经) — Jingfa · Daofa (经法·道法第一)

Focus: the opening chapter of the *Huangdi Sijing* (the Mawangdui silk-manuscript Huang-Lao text), which connects "Dao" to "Fa" (law / standard). Unlike the *Dao De Jing*, which keeps Dao largely in the sphere of personal cultivation, the *Huangdi Sijing* pulls Dao down into statecraft.

- "Dao gives birth to Fa (法). Fa is the line that measures gain and loss and clarifies crooked and straight." The standard is not arbitrary; it is generated from Dao.
- "He who holds the Dao generates Fa and dares not violate it; once Fa is established he dares not abolish it." The lawgiver must first be subject to the law — its authority comes from the lawgiver's own seriousness, not from external force.
- "Void and formless, dim and dark, it is that from which the myriad things are born." Dao is "empty" yet generative — the same "empty vessel" image as *Dao De Jing* Ch.4.
- "The way of seeing-and-knowing is only emptiness-and-presence (虚无有)… once form and name (形名) are established, the distinction of black and white is settled." This is the origin of the *Huangdi Sijing*'s "form-and-name" (形名) doctrine: define things accurately first, and only then can right and wrong be clear.
- "He who holds the Dao, in viewing the world, has no power (埶), no fixed place, no forced action, no selfishness." Four "no's" — the governing posture of one who holds the Dao.

Conceptual hint: the most usable ideas here are (1) *establish form-and-name first* — name and define things accurately so collaboration does not dissipate in vagueness; and (2) *govern from no-self* — the person who stabilizes a system is usually the one who least disturbs it with personal power or private interest.

### Dao De Jing — Chapter 13

Focus: the chapter on "favor and disgrace both startle" and the self as the source of worry.

- "Favor and disgrace are both startling; to prize great trouble is like prizing one's body." The sage opens with a counter-intuitive claim: a person's reactivity to being favored/disgraced is on the same level as their reactivity to bodily danger — both are over-invested.
- "What is meant by 'favor and disgrace are both startling'? Favor is to be beneath. Getting it startles; losing it startles." "Favor" is actually a position of dependence; either way the mind is led by outside judgement — the essence is "self-worth decided by others."
- "The reason I have great trouble is that I have a body. If I had no body, what trouble could I have?" The root of all distress is attachment to the small self.
- "Therefore, only one who prizes the world as his own body may be entrusted with the world; only one who loves the world as his own body may be entrusted with the world." The sage flips "no-self" into "take the world as the body" — not the erasure of self, but the expansion of self to the world, which escapes the petty calculus of gain and loss.

Conceptual hint: most inner turmoil comes from "startled at gaining, startled at losing." The remedy is not "stop caring" but changing the reference point — move attention from "what others think of me" to "whether what I do is worthwhile," and the startle naturally lessens.

### Kṣitigarbha Sutra — Section 13 (Entrusting to Humans and Gods, the final section)

Focus: the closing entrustment and the lists of benefits.

- The Buddha, at the close of the assembly, lays his golden arm on Kṣitigarbha's head and entrusts (付嘱) all future humans and gods to him — filling the gap of the Buddha's absence after parinirvāṇa.
- "Even those who plant the slightest good root — a hair, a mote of dust, a grain of sand, a drop — protect them with your power, let them gradually cultivate the unsurpassed, do not let them retreat." This echoes the sutra's recurring "do not take small goodness lightly."
- In response to Akāśagarbha's question, the Buddha lists "twenty-eight benefits" of seeing, hearing, or honoring Kṣitigarbha — from "celestial dragons protect you" and "good fruits daily increase" all the way to "finally become a Buddha."
- For celestial dragons and spirits, "seven benefits" — swift transcendence, elimination of evil karma, Buddhas' protection, non-retreat from bodhi, increased original power, knowledge of past lives, and finally becoming a Buddha.

Conceptual hint: entrustment is responsibility — to be trusted is to bear the weight of "don't let others fall." And the sutra closes by valuing the small good as much as the small evil: protect even a hair's worth of good root from retreating.
