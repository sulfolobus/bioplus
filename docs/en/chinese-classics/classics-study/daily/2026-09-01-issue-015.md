## Reading guide (English)

### Stomach Meridian (Foot-Yangming) — 仓廪之官

Focus: the meridian that begins where the Large Intestine Meridian leaves off (at the side of the nostril) and runs down the entire front of the body to the second toe, completing the "Large Intestine → Stomach → Spleen" flow.

- "The Stomach meridian of Foot-Yangming begins at the bridge of the nose, alongside the Large Intestine meridian, descends along the outside of the nose, enters the upper teeth, wraps around the mouth and lips, descends to Chengjiang, then along the jaw to Daying, past the cheek and ear to the forehead." It is a primary channel for face-and-mouth conditions — toothache, deviated mouth, facial swelling.
- "A branch from Daying descends through Renying, the throat, into the supraclavicular fossa, passes the diaphragm, belongs to the stomach, and connects with the spleen." This is the anatomical basis of the stomach–spleen paired (outer–inner) relationship.
- "The straight branch descends from the supraclavicular fossa along the inner border of the breast, past the navel, into the qi street (Qichong)." The main trunk runs down the front of the chest and abdomen.
- "A branch from the stomach mouth runs inside the abdomen down to qi street, then to the thigh (Biguan, Futu), through the knee, down the outer shin, to the dorsum of the foot, entering the middle toe." Other branches reach the middle toe's outer side and the great toe — meeting the Spleen Meridian.
- "When diseased: shivering, frequent yawning, dark complexion… fear of people and fire, startled at the sound of wood, wanting to climb high and sing, throw off clothes and run — this is 'arm reversal' (bi jue)." Severe yang-repression signs.
- "It governs diseases of blood: febrile sweating, nasal bleeding, deviated mouth, swollen throat, abdominal edema, knee pain, pain along the chest/breast/thigh/foot, middle-toe immobility." The Stomach meridian is "abundant in both qi and blood."
- "Excess → the whole front of the body is hot, ravenous hunger, yellow urine; deficiency → the front is cold, cold stomach causes distension." The meridian runs along the front of the body — its state shows directly on the "outward-facing" side.

Conceptual hint: the Stomach is the "official of the granary" (仓廪之官). Zusanli (ST-36, below the knee) is the foremost point for strengthening digestion and the whole body ("for abdomen and belly, keep to Zusanli"). The deeper hint: the front of the body is where energy and emotion meet the world — tending the Stomach meridian is tending the side of you that opens outward.

### Dao De Jing — Chapter 15

Focus: the portrait of the "ancient adept" through seven contrasting qualities, ending in the doctrine of "not filling to the brim."

- "The ancient adept: subtle, mysterious, penetrating, too deep to be known." Laozi first sets the overall air of a realized person — not flamboyant, but unfathomable.
- "Because he cannot be known, one can only force a description of him." Seven "xi" similes follow — they describe, not define.
- "Cautious, like crossing a frozen river in winter. Wary, like fearing neighbors on all sides. Grave, like a guest. Dissolving, like ice about to melt. Honest, like uncarved wood. Empty, like a valley. Murky, like turbid water." Severity and warmth, caution and openness, all held together without tilting to one side.
- "Who can, through stillness, make the murky slowly clear? Who can, through long calm, slowly bring forth life?" Not forcing clarity, but nurturing it — "xu" (slowly) is the method.
- "He who holds this Dao does not want to be full. Precisely because he is not full, he can be worn yet newly made (蔽不新成)." The closing point: leave room, and renewal continues.

Conceptual hint: this chapter is about a "maturity that does not show off." The most usable line — "not wanting to be full, therefore able to be worn yet newly made" — is an antidote to today's rush for disruptive, explosive results. Lasting growth is usually slow "xu-qing / xu-sheng" work; leaving blank space is more alive than filling to the brim.

### Huangdi Sijing — Jingfa · Guoci (经法·国次第二)

Focus: the second chapter of the *Huangdi Sijing* (Mawangdui silk manuscript, Huang-Lao text), on the "order of the state" (国次) and the measures of survival and downfall — carrying the "Dao gives birth to Fa" thesis from Chapter 1 into statecraft.

- "When a state loses its order (次), the altars of soil and grain fall into great disorder. To seize without giving, the state will not soon perish [but its roots are wounded]." "Ci" (order) is the legal order established in Daofa — lose it and all goes crooked.
- "Not exhausting the heavenly limit (天极), the declining revive. Punishment and prohibition done improperly, one suffers the calamity instead." Governing hinges on "exhausting the heavenly limit" and on "appropriateness (当)."
- "Forbid and attack only the truly guilty and doomed; lay their state to waste, annex it but do not monopolize it — this is called Heaven's merit (天功)." Merit belongs to Heaven, not to private self.
- "Heaven and Earth are selfless, the four seasons never cease… overstepping the limit and losing what is fit, Heaven will send down calamity. When man forcibly prevails over Heaven, cautiously avoid confronting it; when Heaven prevails over man, go along with it. First yield then extend — exhaust the heavenly limit, but do not appropriate Heaven's merit."
- "To annex another's state and repair its walls, occupy its temples, enjoy its bells, seize its wealth, take its women — this is 'doubly rebellious and wasteful'; the state falls to peril and ruin." The contrast with the sage's way.
- "Only the sage can exhaust the heavenly limit and use Heaven's fitness. The way of Heaven and Earth does not exceed three merits; achievement that does not stop brings peril and calamity." A Huang-Lao version of "achievement completed, then withdraw."
- "The sage's conquest: raze the walls, burn the bells, distribute the wealth, scatter the women, split the land to enfeoff the worthy — this is Heaven's merit. Achievement completed and the law not abandoned, later generations meet no calamity." Same "annexation," opposite outcome: private possession vs. public enfeoffment.
- "No overt surveillance, no covert surveillance, no exhausting the land, no clinging to private power, no factional division — these are the Five Reversals (五逆). When all five reversals form, one disorders Heaven's warp and reverses Earth's web… the mind follows desire, and the body meets calamity — this is 'overstepping the limit and losing what is fit.'" A risk-checklist for organization and person alike.

Conceptual hint: three keywords — **order (次), limit (极), fitness (当)**. Lose the order and the state disorders; exhaust the limit and the declining revive; act improperly and you eat the consequence. The sharpest contrast is "Heaven's merit" vs. "doubly rebellious": the same success should be asked — whose is the merit? For the public or for the private? The Five Reversals read like an organizational risk list. Together with Dao De Jing Ch.15's "not full, therefore newly made," both converge on "leave room" — not overfilled, not over-limited, not over-claimed.
