## Reading guide (English)

### Spleen Meridian (Foot-Taiyin) — 仓廪之副，运化之官

Focus: the meridian that picks up where the Stomach Meridian leaves off (at the great toe), runs up the inner side of the leg, into the abdomen (belongs to the Spleen, connects to the Stomach), and reaches the tongue — the "post-natal foundation" in classical Chinese medicine.

- "The Spleen meridian of Foot-Taiyin begins at the tip of the great toe, runs along the inner white-red border of the toe, past the navicular bone, ascends the front of the inner ankle…" The starting point "great toe" is exactly where the Stomach Meridian's branch ends — the handoff of the qi-flow.
- "…goes up the inside of the calf, behind the shinbone, crossing in front of the Jueyin channel, up the inner front of the knee and thigh, into the abdomen to belong to the Spleen and connect with the Stomach." This is the anatomical basis of the Spleen–Stomach paired (inner–outer) relationship.
- "Ascends past the diaphragm, alongside the throat, connects to the root of the tongue, and spreads under the tongue." The Spleen channel reaches only to the tongue — hence the tongue's appearance (pale, fat, teeth-marked) is a key Spleen sign.
- "A branch separates again from the Stomach, ascends past the diaphragm, and enters the heart." This is the meridian handoff to the Heart Meridian — and the anatomical basis of "Heart–Spleen dual deficiency" patterns.
- "When diseased: stiff tongue root, vomiting after eating, gastric pain, abdominal distension and frequent belching, relieved after passing stool or gas but with a heavy body." Classic Spleen-dampness signs: damp trapped, qi stagnant, the whole body heavy.
- "It governs diseases of the Spleen: tongue root pain, body cannot move, cannot eat down, vexation and tightness below the heart, cold malaria, watery diarrhea or dysentery, urinary retention, jaundice, cannot eat, bluish lips, swelling and pain on the inner thigh and knee when forced to stand, foot great toe unusable." The Spleen's "transform and transport" function failing shows up as damp, stagnation, swelling, yellowing.
- "Excess: the cun-kou pulse is three times larger than the ren-ying pulse; deficiency: the cun-kou is smaller." Cun-kou (the wrist pulse at the yin channel) diagnoses the Spleen directly.

Conceptual hint: the Spleen is the "post-natal foundation" of life. It is not a static organ but a continuous process — "transform, transport, distribute." When you feel heavy after meals, your tongue prints on the sides of your teeth, or you cannot sustain a small daily effort (warm breakfast, slow walk, light dinner), that is usually the Spleen's "movement" stuck. Start gently rather than patching violently — that is the most Spleen-friendly intervention.

### Dao De Jing — Chapter 16

Focus: the foundational chapter on self-cultivation — "attain emptiness, guard stillness," then watch "returning" (复), grasp "constancy" (常), and progress from knowing to encompassing to public-spiritedness to long-lasting safety.

- "Attain utmost emptiness, maintain steadfast stillness." The opening principle: push "emptiness" to the extreme, guard "stillness" firmly. "Attain" (致) means to bring it about, not to wait for it.
- "The ten thousand things arise together; I thereby watch their return." When everything is bustling, observe its "return." The verb "watch" (观) is the method — not intervention, but observation.
- "The myriad things, hustling and bustling, each returns to its root. Returning to the root is called stillness; this is called returning to the mandate (复命)." Movement eventually returns to stillness; arising eventually returns to its root — a necessity.
- "Returning to the mandate is called constancy (常); knowing constancy is called enlightenment (明). Not knowing constancy, one acts recklessly and meets misfortune." The most famous warning in this chapter — six characters: **"not knowing 常, reckless action brings ill."**
- "Knowing constancy, one is encompassing; encompassing, one is public-spirited; public-spirited, one is kingly; kingly, one is heavenly; heavenly, one is with the Dao; with the Dao, one lasts long; one's whole life is without peril." A chain of nine progressions — the ultimate landing is "without peril" (没身不殆).

Conceptual hint: if you only remember one sentence from this chapter, let it be **"不知常，妄作凶"**. Modern people's biggest "reckless action" is to defy the rhythm of constancy — push for overnight disruption, demand immediate returns, want certainty in every step. Doing the reverse of "复命曰常" usually backfires. Before launching or pressing on anything today, ask: is this "constancy" (合规律) or "reckless action" (反规律)? Your answer is usually already written in the moment of your first thought.

### Huangdi Sijing — Jingfa · Junzheng (经法·君正第三)

Focus: the third chapter of the *Huangdi Sijing*, taking the statecraft of "Order of the State" and converting it into an operational seven-year cadence for ruling — "first follow customs, then apply virtue, then let the people gain, then issue orders, then apply punishments, then inspire awe, then be able to mobilize."

- "One year follow their customs; two years use virtue; three years the people gain. Four years issue orders; five years apply punishments; six years the people are awed; seven years can be mobilized." A graded tempo: never skip ahead. Year one to year seven mirrors learning a new skill: connect → nurture → enable → codify → bound → awe → mobilize.
- "Customs = following the people's heart. Virtue = love and encouragement. Gain = relaxing checkpoints and market taxes. Orders = organizing into fives and tens, sorting worthy from unworthy. Punishment = the guilty cannot be pardoned. Mobilization = the people will die for what they hold as honor." Each abstract term gets an operational definition — converting concepts into practice.
- "When orders are issued, pursue them to the end and identify the enemy; one Dao with one heart, above and below do not push against each other, the people have no other intent — only then can one defend and fight." Internal unity must come before external force.
- "Heaven has its times of life and death; the state has its policies of life and death. Following Heaven's giving to nurture life is called 'civil' (文); following Heaven's taking to punish the dead is called 'martial' (武). Civil and martial in parallel, the world obeys." Wen-wu must run together; never one without the other.
- "The foundation of people is the land; of land, fitness (宜); of fitness, timing (时); of timing, the people; of the people, their strength; of their strength, thrift (节)." A six-step deduction, ending with "节" — all force needs to be restrained, or even great strength is wasted.
- "Knowing what suits the land, planting on time, using the people's strength with thrift — then wealth is born. Levies measured, the people are rich. The people rich, they have a sense of shame (有耻). Having shame, orders become custom and punishments go unused." A reverse-causal chain: not "first teach, then shame," but "first fair distribution, then shame arises naturally, then law retreats."
- "Standards (法度) are the utmost of correction (正). Those who govern by standards cannot be disordered. The source of standards cannot be disordered either." Standards must be stable at their source.
- "Precise, public-spirited, selfless, with reliable rewards and punishments — this is how to govern." Four operational virtues.
- "Do not over-demand, levy with restraint, do not rob the people of their seasons — governance is at peace." Three minimalist citizen-protection rails.
- "Without a father's conduct, you cannot obtain the children's service; without a mother's virtue, you cannot exhaust the people's strength. When father-and-mother conduct is complete, it is the virtue of Heaven and Earth." The ruler–people relation as a parent–child pair — strictness (father) and nurturance (mother) both needed.

Conceptual hint: "Junzheng" operationalizes the abstract "次、极、当" of "Guoci." Three takeaways for any modern endeavor — **sequence** (don't skip the seven-year cadence), **parallel** ("文" and "武" together — incentives and constraints must run in parallel), **restraint** ("节" — every force needs a meter). Together with Dao De Jing Ch.16's "not knowing 常, reckless action brings ill," all three converge on one law: respect the order, do not skip, do not over-extend, do not monopolize.
