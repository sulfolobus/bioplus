## Reading guide (English)

### Spleen Meridian (Foot-Taiyin)

Focus: the "transformation and transport" meridian. Its classical "route" starts at the big toe, runs along the inner side of the foot, passes the inner ankle, ascends the inner leg (crossing in front of the Liver meridian on the lower leg), enters the abdomen (where it "belongs to" the spleen and "networks" the stomach), then crosses the diaphragm along the throat, connects to the root of the tongue and spreads beneath it, with a branch that leaves the stomach and enters the heart.

Conceptual hint: in Chinese medicine the spleen is the "root of post-heaven" — the body's energy comes not only from how much one eats, but from how well that food is "transformed" into usable qi and blood. The route ends at the tongue and the heart, which is why digestion problems often show up first as changes in appetite, the lips, or mental focus.

### Dao De Jing — Chapter 4

Focus: the first time the word "道 (Dao)" appears in the text, and it is described by what it is *not* and by how it *works*.

- "The Dao is empty (冲) but using it is never exhausted" — the metaphor of an empty vessel: a full cup can hold no more, but an empty one keeps on being filled. The Dao "works" by leaving room.
- "Deep, as if the ancestor of all things" — Dao is not a thing itself but what everything seems to return to.
- "Blunt the sharp, untie the tangled, soften the light, become one with the dust" — a set of "reverse" moves that lower conflict, complexity, and self-display.
- "I do not know whose child it is; it seems to precede the celestial Emperor" — Dao is deliberately left undefined; the author refuses to nail it down.

### Kṣitigarbha Sutra — Section 4 (Guangmu's vow and karma correspondences)

Focus: the long "background story" of Kṣitigarbha and a list of "karma correspondences".

- The opening parable of two kings: one vows to attain Buddhahood quickly to save the people, the other vows "I will not attain Buddhahood until every suffering being is saved" — that second king is Kṣitigarbha.
- The story of Guangmu (光目): her mother enjoyed eating fish and turtles, killing many lives, and fell into the lower realms. Guangmu makes offerings, paints the Buddha's image, and at last takes a vast vow — "when all beings in the hells and three evil realms have been saved, only then will I become a Buddha." This vow mirrors Kṣitigarbha's own.
- A long list of "karmic correspondences" said by the Buddha: those who kill living beings will meet with short life; those who steal will meet with poverty; those who indulge in improper sexual conduct will meet with separation; those who revile will be reviled in turn; those who are miserly will not have their wishes fulfilled; those who rebel against their parents will meet with natural disasters; and so on through more than twenty entries. The list is a reminder that every small action is being recorded.

Conceptual hint: the section pairs two ideas. (1) **Vow can reshape karma** — Guangmu's vow helps rescue her mother from a karma that seemed already fixed. (2) **Direction matters more than intensity** — a short, one-off good deed is not the same as a long, deep vow that reorganizes one's life. The sutra invites us to ask: what is the *long, deep vow* I am making with my days?
