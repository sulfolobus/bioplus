## Reading guide (English)

### Bladder Meridian (Foot-Taiyang)

Focus: the longest yang meridian of the body, running from the inner canthus of the eye, over the head, down the neck, along both sides of the spine, through the waist and gluteal region, the back of the knee (popliteal fossa), the calf, behind the outer ankle, to the outer side of the little toe.

- The meridian "enters the brain" at the top of the head and emerges at the nape — this is why Bladder Meridian points are commonly used for headaches, neck stiffness, and occipital pain.
- It runs parallel to the spine, connects the kidneys, and "belongs to" the bladder, confirming the interior-exterior (表里) relationship between the kidney and bladder systems.
- With the largest number of acupoints of any meridian, it is sometimes called the body's "back-line defense."

Conceptual hint: problems along this meridian — neck, back, waist, leg — often reflect not a single point of failure but the gradual breakdown of an entire "defense line." Daily habits like prolonged sitting, screen exposure, and cold-damp environments slowly erode it. Maintenance is simple: stretching, back exposure to sunlight, regular movement, and avoiding prolonged cold-damp conditions.

### Dao De Jing — Chapter 7

Focus: the paradox of selflessness leading to self-preservation.

- "Heaven and earth last long. The reason heaven and earth last long is that they do not live for themselves. Thus they can last long." The sun does not shine to "prove itself"; it simply shines. Because there is no self-consuming agenda, it is inexhaustible.
- "Therefore the sage puts himself behind and yet comes to the front; places himself outside and yet is preserved." Not pushing to the front means not blocking others; not obsessing over self-protection means remaining open and responsive.
- "Is it not because he is without self-interest that his self-interest is fulfilled?" The "private" (私) here is not pejorative selfishness but one's own benefit and realization. By serving the larger whole, the individual is naturally nourished.

Conceptual hint: this chapter directly counters the modern reflex of "claim your spot first" and "protect your interests." Sustainable leadership and personal endurance come from embedding oneself in a larger cycle, not from maximum self-assertion.

### Kṣitigarbha Sutra — Section 7 (Benefiting the Living and the Dead)

Focus: what to do at the threshold of death — for the dying person and for the deceased during the forty-nine days after death.

- Kṣitigarbha opens by observing that beings of Jambudvīpa "in every thought and intention, nothing is not sin." This is not meant to induce despair but to establish the depth of habitual patterns that must be acknowledged before they can be transformed.
- The image of "walking in mud, carrying a heavy stone" describes the suffering of those weighed down by karma. A "good knowing advisor" (善知识) who helps lift the burden — even carries it entirely — is of great power. The practical lesson: helping relieve others' burdens is a concrete form of practice.
- At the time of death, family members should create merit for the dying person: hanging banners, lighting lamps, reading sutras, making offerings, or reciting the names of buddhas and bodhisattvas so that the dying person hears them. The key is not the grandeur of the ritual but the sincerity and continuity of the thought.
- During the forty-nine days after death, family members should perform extensive good deeds. Of the merit thus created, the deceased receives one part; the living receive six parts. This means helping the deceased also directly benefits those who remain.
- Kṣitigarbha emphasizes: "While healthy, one should practice for oneself; each portion is fully one's own." Do not postpone self-cultivation until death, relying entirely on others.

Conceptual hint: this section shifts the sutra from cosmic narratives to the most intimate human moment — death. It reframes end-of-life care not as expensive medical intervention but as sincere spiritual support, and reminds us that the best time to practice is now, while we are still healthy.
