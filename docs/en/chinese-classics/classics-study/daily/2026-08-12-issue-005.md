## Reading guide (English)

### Heart Meridian (Hand-Shaoyin)

Focus: the "departing-from-the-organ" meridian. Unlike most meridians that start at the tips of the limbs, the Heart meridian starts inside the chest, "emerging from the heart, belonging to the heart-system (心系), descending through the diaphragm to network the small intestine." From there it sends a branch up along the throat connecting to the eye-system, and a straight branch back up to the lung, out to the armpit, and down the inner-posterior side of the upper arm (running behind the Lung meridian and behind the Pericardium meridian), passing through the inner elbow, the inner-posterior forearm, the wrist bone ("sharp bone" / pisiform), into the palm, and out the inner side of the little finger — where it hands off to the next meridian, the Small Intestine meridian.

Conceptual hint: the route shows that the Heart meridian is fundamentally about "starting from the centre." The Chinese medicine framing is that the heart "stores the spirit" (心藏神), so the meridian's pattern of starting inside the chest, connecting upward to the throat and eye-system, and downward to the small intestine is read as the body's "centre-to-periphery" map for consciousness and digestion. The luo-connecting vessel at 通里 (Tongli) "enters the heart, connects to the root of the tongue, and belongs to the eye-system" — the anatomical root of "the heart opens into the tongue" (心开窍于舌).

### Dao De Jing — Chapter 5

Focus: the chapter on "Heaven-and-earth are not benevolent" and the image of the bellows.

- "Heaven-and-earth are not benevolent; they treat the ten thousand things as straw dogs." Dao does not show partiality. "Straw dogs" are the straw effigies used at sacrifices — handled with care during the ritual, then discarded. The metaphor is "used-without-attachment," not "devalued."
- "The sage is not benevolent; he treats the people as straw dogs." By the same logic, a ruler who lets things take their natural course avoids the distortion that personal favourites always bring.
- "The space between heaven and earth is like a bellows: empty, yet never exhausted; the more it moves, the more it produces." Continues Chapter 4's "empty-yet-not-full" image, this time as a working machine.
- "Words are many; exhaustion comes quickly. Better to hold to the centre." Trying to master everything through speech hits its limit fast; it's better to remain "in the middle" — not eccentric, not partial.

Conceptual hint: the chapter is not advocating cold-heartedness. It is describing a stance in which personal partiality is removed so that the natural course of things can unfold. Applied to self-governance and team dynamics: rules that outlast personal preference produce steadier outcomes; an inner life that allows "empty space" lets new things keep arriving.

### Kṣitigarbha Sutra — Section 5 (Names and karma-reports of the hells)

Focus: a "place-name and instrument" rendering of karma. After Section 4 listed abstract karmic correspondences, Section 5 turns them into a long roll-call of hell locations.

- Samantabhadra Bodhisattva asks Kṣitigarbha to name the hells so that future beings in the "latter-day dharma" might know the fruit of karma.
- Kṣitigarbha lists the great hells inside the Iron Ring Mountain (铁围山): the supreme "Avīci" (极无间), "Great Avīci" (大阿鼻), and a torrent of named hells — Horned, Flying Blade, Fire Arrow, Squeezing Mountain, Lance, Iron Cart, Iron Bed, Iron Ox, Iron Garment, Thousand Blades, Iron Donkey, Molten Copper, Embrace-the-Pillar, Flowing Fire, Tongue-Plough, File-the-Head, Burn-the-Feet, Eye-Biter, Iron Pellet, Quarrel, Iron Axe, and so on; then a second list of calling-hells, tongue-pulling-hells, dung-and-urine-hells, fire-elephant/dog/horse/ox hells, fire-mountain / fire-stone / fire-bed / fire-beam / fire-eagle hells, saw-tooth, flaying, blood-drinking, hand-burning, foot-burning, thorn-hell, fire-house, iron-house, fire-wolf hells, with "each containing its own small hells, numbering from one or two to hundreds of thousands."
- The key sentence of the section: "Therefore, beings — do not take small evils lightly, thinking them guiltless. After death there is retribution; it is received down to the very fibre." (莫轻小恶，以为无罪，死后有报，纤毫受之。)
- "Father and son are the closest of kin, yet at the fork of the road each walks his own way; even if they meet, none will bear it for another." Reinforces that no one inherits your karmic ledger — but does not contradict the Guangmu story, because Guangmu's aid came through a vow that redirected karma rather than taking it on.

Conceptual hint: the place-name list is essentially a meditation aid. It is not a tourism guide to the underworld, but a way of taking "do not take small evils lightly" out of the abstract and into the concrete. Its contemporary application is "every micro-action is being recorded" — a quiet reminder that today is made of small moments, and those small moments are not nothing.
